Part 1 — The context

Gifting in Nigeria is large, recurring, emotionally loaded, and almost entirely un-productised.

Today the flow looks like this: someone remembers a birthday late, drops a message in a WhatsApp group, asks for an account number, sends ₦20,000, and the moment is over. Or they panic-buy something generic on the day. The money moves. The meaning mostly doesn't.

Three facts frame the opportunity:

Nigeria received $21.8 billion in diaspora remittances in 2025, effectively flat against 2024's $21.81 billion, according to CBN data. A meaningful share of that is occasion-driven — birthdays, weddings, naming ceremonies, school fees, Christmas. It moves as cash because there is no good alternative.

Jumia's Nigeria business grew GMV 50% year-on-year in Q4 2025 with orders up 33%. Nigerian e-commerce is not stagnant; it is compounding.

Jumia's group GMV for full-year 2025 was $818.6 million on revenue of $188.9 million, with an operating loss of $63.2 million. Scale in this market is achievable. Profitable scale takes a decade.

The gap is not supply. Jumia, Temu and Amazon already solve "I know what I want." Nobody solves "I know who I love and when, but I don't know what to give, and I'm afraid of getting it wrong."

That is the business.

## Part 2 — What we're actually building


Givtme is an occasion-intent platform with an e-commerce engine underneath.

The distinction matters commercially, not just semantically:

That last row is the whole moat. Jumia can copy a wishlist feature in a sprint. It cannot copy the fact that 40,000 Nigerians have told us their mother's birthday and their partner's shoe size.

The core interaction — the unit that everything else must serve — is: a giver and a receiver coordinate around a date. Every feature either makes that interaction happen more often, more easily, or with more people involved. Anything that doesn't is a distraction.

This is the discipline Sangeet Paul Choudary argues for in Platform Scale (and with Parker and Van Alstyne in Platform Revolution): define the core interaction, remove friction from it, then build the pull, facilitation and matching layers around it. You referenced the book — this is the part to actually apply.

### The three pillars, and what each one is really for


Note what the current build is missing against this. The live site is a competent conventional gift store: occasions, curated boxes at ₦25,000–₦30,000, a cart, a wishlist page. What it does not yet have is the thing that makes it a platform — reminders that fire, links that unfurl, wishlists that get shared, and pooling that lets six people buy one gift. Those four are the product. The store is the fulfilment layer.

## Part 3 — How Givtme makes money


Seven revenue lines, sequenced by when they can realistically be switched on.

### Live from day one


1. First-party curated boxes (own inventory) What you sell now. Gross margin 30–40%. Highest margin per order, but inventory risk and the hardest to scale. Keep this narrow — six to ten signature boxes that define the brand — and let the marketplace carry breadth.

2. Marketplace commission (third-party vendors) Cakes, flowers, hampers, perfume, jewellery, skincare, small appliances, experiences. Target 12–20% take rate, higher than Jumia's blended rate because gifting buyers are deadline-driven and comparison-shop far less. Vendor acquisition in Lagos is relationship-led and fast — this is where your existing network is an unfair advantage.

3. Delivery & occasion services Same-day and timed delivery, gift wrapping, handwritten card, "deliver at 9am on the day." Charge for precision. A gift that arrives the day after a birthday is worth nothing — which means people will pay a premium for certainty in a way they never would for a phone charger.

### Turn on in months 3–9


4. Gift pooling (the underpriced one) Six friends contributing ₦15,000 each toward one ₦90,000 gift. Charge 3–5% platform fee on pooled funds, payment processing passed through separately.

This is strategically the strongest line, because it does three things at once: raises effective order value dramatically, pulls 5–15 new users into the platform per pool, and makes Givtme the place money for celebrations sits rather than just the place gifts are bought.

Important: do not hold customer funds on your own balance sheet. Pooled contributions must sit with a licensed partner (Paystack, Flutterwave, or a licensed escrow provider) under their regulatory cover. Getting this wrong is an existential risk, not a compliance detail. Take legal advice before a single naira is pooled — I'm not qualified to give it.

5. Diaspora corridor This is, in my view, your sharpest wedge and you have not emphasised it enough.

A Nigerian in London wants to do something for her mother's 60th in Ikeja. Today she sends £200 and hopes. Givtme lets her pay in GBP, choose the gift, see the delivery photo, and include a note. Revenue comes from product margin + premium delivery + FX spread, and diaspora order values run 2–4x local ones.

It also reframes the pitch entirely: you are not competing with Jumia for Lagos wallet share, you are converting a slice of a $21.8 billion remittance flow from cash into curated goods. That is a much bigger and much more fundable story.

### Turn on in year 1–2


6. Corporate gifting — and this should be your revenue backbone in year one You described it yourself: "I have my boss, my colleagues, my employees. I want to remember all the birthdays." That is a product with a buyer, a budget line, and no meaningful incumbent in Nigeria.

HR teams buying employee birthday and work-anniversary gifts. Account managers sending client festive hampers. Pricing: subscription per employee per year (₦3,000–₦8,000) plus product margin, or pure product margin on an annual contract.

One corporate account with 200 staff is worth roughly the same as 120 consumer orders, closes in weeks, and renews annually. Consumer builds the brand and the graph; corporate pays the salaries while it does. Run both from month one.

### Year 2–3, not before


7. Retail media, ads and data Banner placements, sponsored listings in Gift Ideas, promoted vendor slots, category sponsorships (Sallah, Detty December, Valentine's), and anonymised gift-intent reports sold to consumer brands.

Be realistic about timing. Jumia's advertising revenue is still only about 1% of GMV even at $818m scale — they themselves flag it as upside yet to be captured. Ads need seller density and traffic volume you will not have for 24 months. Name it in the pitch as the year-three margin story. Do not put it in the year-one model.

## Part 5 — Twelve-month model


### Base case


Cost base assumes roughly ₦10–14m/month: engineering and product (3), operations and customer care (2), growth and content (2), leadership, tooling and infrastructure, plus ₦3–4m/month marketing.

The quarterly profitability above is contribution-level and fragile. Treat any Q3/Q4 surplus as reinvestment fuel, not profit. Realistically, plan for the full year to land between (₦40m) and break-even once hiring ahead of growth, seasonal inventory and bad-debt surprises are absorbed.

## Part 6 — On the Jumia target


You said one-third of Jumia's financial capacity within a year. I want to be straight with you, because this number will come up in an investor room and it needs to survive contact.

Jumia's 2025 numbers: GMV $818.6 million, revenue $188.9 million. One-third means roughly $273m GMV or $63m revenue — about ₦410 billion and ₦95 billion respectively. That is 450x the base case above, in twelve months, against a company that has spent thirteen years and hundreds of millions of dollars in losses building warehouses, a delivery fleet, a payments licence and a seller base across nine countries.

It will not happen in a year. No company in African e-commerce has ever done it, and saying it out loud costs you credibility with exactly the people you need.

Here's the version that is both ambitious and defensible:

Five years of aggressive, well-funded, largely uninterrupted growth gets you to roughly 10% of Jumia's current GMV — and by then Jumia will be bigger too.

But notice what that path actually contains: a business doing $80 million of GMV at 20%+ take rate with a genuine network effect and proprietary relationship data. On gifting economics, that company is worth considerably more per dollar of GMV than Jumia is, because Jumia carries the entire cost of general merchandise logistics at 12% gross margin on GMV.

You don't need to be one-third of Jumia. You need to be worth more per naira than Jumia. That is a winnable argument and a much better pitch.

## Part 8 — The next ninety days


Settle the name and register the domain. This week.

Ship the four platform features — reminders that fire, link unfurling, shareable public wishlists, gift pooling. The store already works; this is what makes it a platform.

Sign 10 corporate accounts. Start with people who already know you. This is revenue you can close without a marketing budget.

Recruit 25 marketplace vendors across cakes, flowers, hampers and perfume.

Test the diaspora corridor with 50 orders from the UK and US. If the economics hold, it moves to the centre of the strategy.

Instrument the loop — shares per wishlist, opens per share, signups per open, orders per signup. Review weekly.

Run one cultural moment before December. Detty December is the single largest gifting window in the Nigerian calendar and it is already on the horizon.

Figures in this document are modelled assumptions built for decision-making, not audited forecasts. Jumia and CBN figures are drawn from published results and central bank data.
