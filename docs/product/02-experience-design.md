## Part 1 — The context


Nobody wakes up wanting to use a gifting app.

What they wake up with is a low-grade dread: my sister's birthday is somewhere this month and I don't know what to get her. It sits at the back of the mind for two weeks. Then the day arrives, and they send money with an apology attached, or they buy something safe and forgettable, and the feeling that follows isn't joy. It's relief that it's over.

That feeling — the anxiety, not the transaction — is what Givtme is designed against.

Duolingo didn't win because it taught languages better. It won because it understood that the real enemy was quitting, and it built its entire product around one emotion. Our enemy is gift anxiety, and it comes in three forms.

### The three fears


Every screen in this product should be traceable back to one of those three lines. If it isn't, it's clutter.

### And the fear on the other side


Receiving has its own awkwardness, and it's badly underserved. Nigerians are direct about many things and shy about this one. Nobody wants to say buy me this. A wishlist reframes the ask as an invitation — you're not demanding, you're making it easy for people who already want to celebrate you. That reframe is the entire emotional permission structure behind the feature. The copy has to carry it.

## Part 2 — The objectives


The experience has to achieve four things. In order:

Turn gifting from an event into a system. The user sets it up once and it runs. This is what converts a shopping app into a utility.

Remove the blank page. The user should never face an empty search bar. They should face a suggestion that already knows something about the person.

Make thoughtfulness visible. People share what makes them look good. Being seen as the thoughtful one is the social currency this product trades in — and the reason anyone shares anything.

Make the moment better than the object. The gift is a ₦30,000 candle set. The experience is the reveal, the note, the photo of her face. Compete on the second one, because that's where Jumia can't follow.

## Part 3 — The cultural layer


This is the part most Nigerian products get wrong in one of two directions: either they're a Silicon Valley template with a Nigerian flag in the footer, or they overcorrect into pidgin-as-decoration, which reads as a brand performing Nigerianness at people rather than speaking to them.

The rule: be specific rather than loud. Specificity is what signals you actually live here.

### The occasion calendar is the proof


A generic gifting app offers birthdays, weddings, anniversaries. That's what the current site does. Ours should recognise:

Naming ceremony (the eighth day) — and know that the gift goes to the mother as much as the baby

Omugwo — grandmother's arrival, a distinct moment with its own etiquette

Introduction, traditional, and white wedding — three separate occasions, three different gift registers, not one "wedding"

Detty December — homecoming season, the largest gifting window in the calendar

Sallah — Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, with proper regional awareness

Japa send-off — someone is leaving. Practical gifts, emotional weight.

NYSC passing out, convocation, first job, promotion, new house, new car

Val's, Mother's Day, Father's Day, Children's Day

Condolence and sympathy — handled separately and with care. See the rule at the end of this section.

A user who opens the occasion list and sees Omugwo sitting there understands immediately that this was built by someone who knows. That single moment of recognition does more brand work than a month of advertising.

### Voice


Warm, specific, slightly knowing. Never generic, never trying too hard.

That last one matters more than it looks. A product that only celebrates success is exhausting. One that helps you recover from failure earns loyalty.

### The mascot


Duolingo's owl works because it owns one emotion (guilt) attached to one behaviour (maintaining the streak). It is not a decoration; it is a mechanic with a face.

The brief for ours, rather than a finished answer — this should be developed with a proper character designer:

One emotion: anticipation. Not guilt. Guilt is the wrong register for love, and it will make people delete the app. Our character is excited on your behalf — it knows something good is coming and it can barely keep it in.

One behaviour: don't let the date pass. Every appearance is tied to an upcoming occasion.

Survives at 32 pixels, flat, in one colour. If it needs gradients and detail to read, it's the wrong design.

Works as a WhatsApp sticker pack. This is not a nice-to-have. WhatsApp is where Nigerian social life happens, and a sticker pack that spreads through family groups is close to free distribution. Design for it from the first sketch.

Has a name people can say. Not "the Givtme mascot."

A shape worth exploring: a small wrapped parcel with legs, a character carrying something behind its back, or a figure that only exists in the days before an occasion and vanishes after. The disappearing-after-the-date idea is interesting because it gives scarcity to the character's presence.

### The one hard rule


Grief is not gamified. Condolence gifting is real, common, and culturally significant in Nigeria. When a user selects sympathy or bereavement, the mascot disappears. No streaks, no confetti, no badges, no "you're on fire." Typography goes quieter, the palette drops to muted tones, the copy becomes plain and short.

Getting this right will be noticed and remembered. Getting it wrong is the kind of mistake that gets screenshotted and ends a brand.

## Part 4 — The journey, as the user feels it


### Arrival


Most users will not arrive at a homepage. They will arrive at a friend's wishlist, sent in a WhatsApp chat. That page is the real front door and should be designed as the most important screen in the product.

It has to do four things in about eight seconds: show whose list it is and what's coming up, let the visitor act without an account, make the visitor feel something about the person, and plant the thought I should make one of these.

The signup prompt does not appear first. It appears after they've chosen a gift — at the moment of highest warmth, framed as an offer rather than a toll: "Want us to remind you next year?"

### Setting up


The setup moment decides retention, and it has to be light. Import contacts or add two or three people manually. For each, a name, a date, and the relationship. Nothing else. Then — and this is the important bit — show value before asking for anything more: as soon as the first date is saved, surface three gift ideas for that person. The user should see the product work before they've invested in it.

The wrong version of this screen asks for full profile setup, preferences, budget ranges and notification settings before anything happens. That screen loses 70% of people.

### Waiting


Most of the relationship with this product is silence, and silence is where apps die.

The reminder ladder, with a strict cap of three per occasion:

30 days out: quiet, planning-oriented. Big occasions only. "Your mum turns 60 in a month. Worth planning properly."

7 days out: the real one. Carries three specific suggestions based on relationship, past gifts, and their wishlist if they have one.

1 day out: urgent, single-tap. Same-day delivery options only. No browsing — decisions.

Channel should be the user's choice: WhatsApp, push, email. In Nigeria, WhatsApp will outperform push by a wide margin. Build for it first.

### Choosing


The blank search bar is the enemy. Entry points, in order of preference:

Their wishlist — if it exists, nothing else competes. Show it first, show it big.

The Gift Museum — the phrase is already on the site and it's good. Curated, browsable, organised by relationship and feeling rather than by category. "For someone who's had a hard year." "For a mother who says she doesn't need anything." Not "Electronics under ₦50,000."

What people like them are asking for — aggregated wishlist demand. Genuinely useful, and it's a data asset nobody else has.

Paste a link — from anywhere. Pulls in the item card.

Each item should carry the human context: "Asked for by 340 people this month." "Most-given gift for mothers in September." That's evidence over assertion — it does more work than any copy claiming the product is thoughtful.

### The ask, for the receiver


Creating a wishlist has to feel like generosity, not greed. Frame it as helping people who already want to celebrate you. Let items be added from anywhere. Let the list be public, link-only, or private. Let items be marked as claimed so two people don't buy the same thing — quietly, without the owner seeing who claimed what, so the surprise survives.

Then make the shared list beautiful enough to post. If it looks good on a WhatsApp status and an Instagram story, it travels. If it looks like a spreadsheet, it dies in the chat.

### Pooling


The moment that turns one buyer into eight users.

Someone starts a pool, sets a target, shares a link. Contributors pay any amount — no minimum, because a minimum is a barrier and an embarrassment. A live wall shows who has contributed, with an option to contribute quietly. When the target is hit, everyone gets notified together.

The contributor wall is the screenshot. Design it for that.

### The moment itself


This is the whole product, and it's the part most easily neglected because it happens after the money has been taken.

The gift arrives on the day, in the window promised. Non-negotiable.

It's wrapped properly. The packaging is the first thing they see, and it is doing brand work.

The note is there, in the giver's words.

The giver gets a delivery photo. For diaspora users, this is the single most valuable thing the product does. Someone in Manchester sees their mother holding the box in Surulere. Nothing else in the experience comes close to that.

The receiver gets a light, un-pushy prompt to say thank you — which closes the loop and, incidentally, brings them into the platform as a user.

### Afterwards


A giving history. Not a purchase history — a record of thoughtfulness. What you gave, to whom, when. It prevents repeats and quietly builds attachment.

A gentle streak. "Eleven months without missing a date." Framed as a fact about the user, never as pressure. The moment it becomes a threat, it's Duolingo's owl and it's wrong for this product.

Givtme Wrapped, every December. Your giving year: how many people you remembered, who you gave to most, the gift that landed best. Shareable, beautiful, ego-flattering in the right way. This is Spotify's mechanic and it's the correct one to borrow — it's built entirely from data the user gave you, and it arrives exactly when the biggest gifting season starts.

## Part 5 — The moments people will screenshot


Every product that spreads has three or four images that travel without it. Design these deliberately:

The shared wishlist page — branded, personal, good-looking on a phone

The contributor wall when a pool completes

The delivery photo with the giver's note beside it

Givtme Wrapped

The occasion list itself — the first time someone sees Omugwo in a dropdown, they will show someone

## Part 6 — Design identity


Your instinct runs toward restraint, evidence over assertion, premium quietness. A mascot-led cultural brand needs more warmth than that register naturally allows. The resolution is to split it:

Playful in the emotional layer — reminders, mascot, empty states, Wrapped, pool celebrations, sticker packs. This is where the cultural relevance lives and where people share.

Restrained in the commercial layer — checkout, payment, receipts, delivery tracking, corporate accounts. When money moves, the interface should feel like a bank, not a toy. Nigerian users have well-founded trust concerns with new payment surfaces, and playfulness at checkout reads as amateur.

The brands you cited all do exactly this. Duolingo's owl never appears on the subscription payment screen.

Practical notes: design mobile-first and Android-first — that's the market, not an afterthought. Assume unreliable connections; every screen needs a graceful low-bandwidth state. Test on a mid-range Android phone in poor signal before anything ships, because that's where your user actually is.

## Part 7 — What success feels like


Not "users grew 40%."

It feels like this: someone's colleague says "How did you know it was my mum's birthday?" and they answer "Givtme told me." And the colleague pulls out their phone right there.

That sentence — Givtme told me — is the product working. Everything in this document exists to make it more likely.

Written to sit alongside the business case and the user journey. The business case says why this is worth building. This says what it should feel like. The user journey says how to build it.
